# Roles in Multiplayer Games Are Design Debt

I've been an avid follower of League of Legends since Season 2 and it's been fascinating to see the trajectory of its evolution for over 10 years and how the designers face its various problems. However, in the past couple years, the biggest issue that seems to have plagued the designers time and time again has been the problem of defined roles.

For some quick history, the roles of LoL top, mid, jungle, ADC, and support simply did not exist when the game first arrived. Even when I first started playing, seeing 2 people top and no jungler was a common sight in lower elos. It wasn't until Fnatic, the first ever World Champions, would come to define "the Fnatic Meta" which was solo top, solo mid, jungler, and support/AD Carry roles that would define League of Legends to this day. Even the fact that it was called a "meta" sort of implied that this was never supposed to be codified, but rather simply the strongest arrangement of players at the time. 

But back before role selection or even Team Builder as it used to be called, you simply loaded into a lobby and called your role. Back in the day, even the number of laners per lane wasn't even defined. Thus, the term "solo mid" was created, leading to the forum solomid.net and eventually to one of the most famous North American esports franchises, Team SoloMid (TSM). Yeah, I'm an unc by League standards already, I know.

But going back to the present day 2026, there are the introduction of Role Quests which are meant to force the players into their specific roles by giving them rewards for doing somethign they would already be doing in that role, and by actively punishing for not fulfilling their role. 

If you've queued for top lane, you will be given a quest that when fulfillled will give you a strong buff, and these quests are designed in such a way that they can only be filled by their laner. That way, you are encouraged not to move between lanes, and to stay within your role. 

This was introduced mainly as a fix to a strategy where the laners would rotate between all the lanes in order to avoid bad matchups, which was perceived to lead to stale and frustrating gameplay. Before these were introduced, minor changes would be introduced as fixes to previous laneswap metas, such as increasing turret health or turret gold distribution. From there, we should have the breathing room to just design the next champions elegantly so they fulfill only the roles we want them to, right?

Even with these heavy handed changes, AD Carries champions which relied on auto attacks are being played in other lanes, and their traditional roles are being supplanted by spell-based mages. Supports are roaming around the map, and utilizing their quest EXP to slingshot them back into the game. And once again, we are back where we have started of needing to force players back into their roles. 

These balance woes have all stemmed from a major decision to codify the Fnatic Meta into the fabric of competitive League of Legends. While it took some time before people realized the Fnatic Meta was no longer the strongest arrangement of players, once the first lane swap meta entered professional play, it became a force to reckon with.

## So what are some ways we can try and avoid these problems in the first place, and what are some tradeoffs that we make when doing so?

Well first, it's important to understand why roles exist in the first place. It's easy to say that this problem never would have happened had Riot Games never codified the Fnatic Meta in the first place, but the reality is that roles are an inevitable part of any team game. Even games like soccer (or football for any non-Americans reading this) have defined roles such as strikers, wings, defense, and even special rules for the goalie. Since people, characters, or champions all have different abilities, naturally people will gravitate to playing the game in whatever manner they find most fun, appealling, or just whatever they happen to be best at. 

Roles are an important part of a team activity, as it distills the jobs of a player down into meaningful archetypes that can help onboard a player quickly. What those roles are happen to usually fall into a combination of what's the strongest and what's most digestible. For instance, a football defender has a pretty straightforward job that at the end of the day sort of just boils down into "get the ball away from the net". 

Roles in League of Legends were important in that it removed the need to call your roles as soon as you arrived in lobby. This often meant that if you lived closer to the servers and/or had fast internet you got to play your favorite role, and if not then you'd better start learning support or jungle. 

By codifying the role, it alleviated a really bad player experience of needing to call your role, but introduced a litanny of design problems in and out of the game that the Riot team is struggling to pay off still to this day. Some form of roles of were likely inevitable, but we can look to other games to see how they deal with them. 

### Be very careful when you create less resource locations than players 

In League of Legends each lane gets waves of minions that give gold to a player whenever they deal the killing blow or the "last hit" to them. This means that in a minion wave, there is a finite amount of gold that can be given to each player, and having multiple players trying to go for last hits will often result in the detriment of both. 

However, in League of Legends there are 4 players that go into lanes, but only 3 lanes where these minions spawn. This is why supports (which traditionally share a lane with the AD Carry in the bottom lane) were given special items designed specificially for them even very early on in the game specifically to supplement their income to make up for their lack of gold. 

If we imagine a world in which there were 4 players per team, I believe that the need for such defined roles would be greatly reduced. Rather, each lane would just have their lane opponent to face off with. The possibility of switching lanes would be more palatable to play against, and it would be very difficult to see much disruption of player distribution. I don't necessarily think it would be more fun as a support main myself, but I think it shows how certain distributions of resources let somebody stumble into a more codified game meta than they might initially have hoped for. 

### Base your game around a centralizing and universal mechanic:

We can funnily enough, look to Valorant which is Riot's tactical shooter to see how this shapes out. In Valorant, every single character has multiple abilities similarly to League of Legends, but the difference is that they all have the same exact guns. Every single character can buy the same guns and use the same weaponry, and the main way to interact in the game is indeed through the gunplay rather than the abilities. 

Roles DO exist in Valorant depending on what the abilities each character has specializes in, but they aren't codified into the fabric of the game as you can choose any number and combination of different roles. 

Valorant (and many other shooters) can get away with this because their universal guns are what the game is centralized around, thus individual character differences aren't as game defining as they are in League of Legends.

Despite also being an FPS, every character is extremely different and thus ran into pretty much the exact same problems as League of Legends to the point of even changing the team sizes due to how overpowered one of their classes were. Overwatch doesn't even have limited resources and doesn't have to deal with a zero-sum resource game like League of Legends, and yet still suffered from this by sheer class power imbalances. 

### The more specific classes you have, the harder your life will become

It isn't necessarily bad to have a role only sequestered into one location, so long as there's a "natural" reason for that role to exist. Take for instance, jungling in League of Legends. Rather than fight the minions that regularly spawn, there is a forest between the lanes in which there are monsters that similarly give resources. Naturally, a jungler role emerged as it was more efficient to have somebody fully dedicated to getting resources from these monsters, rather than sharing lane resources with another person (but even this had has some strange metagames with regards to sometimes having multiple junglers and other game-breaking possibilities). The map design lent itself to a role that was defined merely by where they went and what they did without having to put in external and opaque rules to shoehorn players into their places. 

Probably one of the classes that gives the most grief is AD Carry. While other roles are defined merely by their location, AD Carry is specifically designed for auto-attack based champions to exist. While this has more recently moved to being called Bot[om] lane, the vast majority of players in this role specialize in these auto attack champions and usually enjoy playing these champions exclusively. 

## Classes are hard, but can be very rewarding when designed well

The more specialized your classes are, the more problems you will likely eventually have to face because you are essentially fracturing your playerbase into different segments. Inevitably some of those segments will have periods of strength sometimes at the detriment of the others. This may give you cause to implement out of game matchmaking based on these segments and codify them into the game to improve player out of game experience. But often those defined segments didn't actually have to exist within the context of a competitive metagame in the first place. Instead they were merely a transient meta that you as the deveoper have now locked into the game, and players have come to expect. 

Just because something carries much design debt, doesn't necessarily mean you shouldn't have them. Having a role is what it means to live in a society, as we literally "have a role in society." Without any defined role, the players will inevitably come into conflict when you allow them to fight for the roles without any matchmaking. When a team of players come together to become more than the some of their individual parts, it can be some of the most rewarding experiences multiplayer games have to offer.

It's important then to define them specifically enough to capture the essence of what it might mean to play that role, but not so specific where you have to begin creating extra rules to artificially keep the roles in place.
